A generative game simulator for counterfactual baseball

Projection systems answer how good is a player. They cannot answer what happens in this game, because a game is a correlated sequence of plate appearances, not a sum of independent rates. DiamondWorld plays the game out, one plate appearance at a time.

DiamondWorld — a plate-appearance-level generative model (JAX / NumPyro SVI), an empirical rules engine, and a full game simulator. Trained 2015–2023, evaluated on 2024.

What-if

1 · Counterfactual analysis

Hold a real 2024 game fixed, change one input, and re-simulate. These are causal estimates from replaying the same game, not correlations from a season of noise.

One competitive 2024 game (baseline win prob near 0.5), R = 400 replicas per scenario.
InterventionHome win prob.ChangeExp. home runs
Baseline0.443—4.49
Home starter → ace (Ohtani)0.540+9.8 pts4.35
Home starter → replacement (Pelfrey)0.480+3.7 pts*4.86
Weakest hitter → star bat (Judge)0.555+11.3 pts5.39 (+0.90)

An ace lifts this game about 10 points of win probability; a single elite bat is worth about 11 points and nearly a full run. The replacement-starter row (*) sits within simulation noise of the baseline, because the actual home starter was already a fringe arm, so a lateral downgrade has little to move — the causal estimate tracks the real talent gap, not the label. The same machinery answers roster and trade-deadline questions, lineup-card decisions, and injury-replacement impact: change the input, replay the game, read the effect.

Small, as sabermetrics has long held, but real and free: over a season it is a win or two from a decision that costs nothing. A smarter optimizer plugs into the same simulator.

Tail risk

3 · Correlated tail-risk

A game is not the sum of independent player outcomes; runs cluster (a walk, a single, a homer in one inning). Per-player systems, even summed, model the mean but not the shape. From the baseline game's full simulated distribution:

Full outcome distribution, one game, from the generative simulator.
QuantityValue
Mean total runs9.52
P(total ≥ 10)0.465
P(total ≤ 5)0.185
P(shutout by either side)0.083
P(blowout, margin ≥ 5)0.250
Game-total variance vs independent-Poisson18.85 vs 9.52  (1.98×)

That 1.98× overdispersion is exactly the within-game correlation a summed-projection model misses. It matters for anything tail-sensitive: bullpen deployment, blowout and leverage planning, distribution-aware win expectancy.

Uncertainty

4 · Win probability with honest uncertainty

Most win-probability models return one number. A generative model returns a distribution, and separates two kinds of uncertainty: aleatoric (the game could play out many ways) and epistemic (how sure we are about the rosters). We draw K = 4 realizations from the fitted player-skill posterior and simulate R = 100 replicas of each; the decomposition below is over 200 games, the calibration over all 2,376 completed 2024 games.

Decomposed uncertainty in pre-game win probability.
SourceSpread (WP)
Epistemic — roster / skill uncertainty4.6%
Aleatoric — single-game randomness (R = 100)4.9%

Roster uncertainty is comparable to single-game sampling noise, so a point win probability throws away half the story: two games reading “0.62” can carry very different credible bands. The interval, not the point, is the honest output for bet sizing and leverage.

Is the point estimate calibrated? Now measured over the full season.

An earlier 30-game slice looked over-confident toward the home team, but it was small and home-unlucky. Over all 2,376 completed 2024 games the point win probability is well calibrated: ECE 0.039, Brier 0.247 (vs a 0.250 always-base-rate reference), and the model's mean win probability (0.518) matches the actual home-win base rate (0.521) almost exactly — with only a mild residual over-confidence on strong home favorites (0.596 predicted vs 0.565 observed). The reliability curve hugs the diagonal.

Grounding

Are the players any good?

For the game-level analyses to mean anything, the players inside the simulator have to be realistic. We benchmark against Marcel, the reproducible public baseline, and against Steamer's actual preseason-2024 projections, recovered from a public archive. The claim that professional systems beat Marcel "by a few points" is folklore we previously had to lean on; it is worth exactly 0.045 of average correlation. v16 sits 0.015 below Marcel and 0.060 below Steamer, its largest single deficit still hit rate. The players are realistic enough that the simulator's counterfactual, lineup, tail, and uncertainty results are grounded, but they are not a match for a dedicated projection system, and we no longer have to guess by how much. Marcel and Steamer are scored on the 376 batters both cover, DiamondWorld on its own larger set; restricting Marcel to the common set moves it by less than 0.001, so the populations are the same and the comparison transfers. ZiPS and THE BAT are absent because no usable preseason-2024 capture of either exists. Rather than estimate them, we report only what can be sourced.

Where the remaining gap lives

Because architecture barely matters here (every discriminative net lands near 0.577), the gap has to be inputs. v15's entire player prior is five numbers per batter: recency-weighted strikeout, walk, hit and home-run rates, plus a plate-appearance count. That is Marcel's information set without Marcel's tuning. Two levers, built from raw data already on disk, recover most of the difference:

Levers applied to a Marcel-style projection, 2024 test set.
VariantHit %HR %Average
Marcel, one regression constant for all stats0.4180.6080.626
+ per-stat tuned regression0.4280.6080.627
+ contact quality (xBA-style)0.4970.6410.652
Steamer0.5100.6510.671

A hit rate built from outcomes carries a season of ball-in-play luck. Scoring a batter on the contact they made instead, by binning every batted ball on exit velocity and launch angle and taking the league hit frequency in that bin, moves hit-rate correlation from 0.418 to 0.497, nearly the entire deficit against Steamer. The tuned regression constants are their own diagnostic: strikeouts stabilise at 200 plate appearances of regression, walks at 400, hits and home runs at 2200. A single constant for all four, which is what we shipped, is wrong at both ends. Together the levers recover 58% of the Marcel-to-Steamer gap; the remaining 0.019 is age curves and minor-league translation, neither derivable from pitch data.

This was first measured on a Marcel-style projection, establishing the information exists and is usable. We then shipped it: v16 = v15 with the contact-quality feature reaches 0.611 (+0.017), about two thirds of what the projection-level test predicted, with the gain landing in hit and home runs exactly as designed. So the model does exploit the signal. It also retracts an earlier conclusion of ours that the model was "input-saturated": that test fed summary descriptors such as mean exit velocity alongside the noisy rates, pooled over eight seasons, which removes the very noise expected statistics exist to correct. The ceiling we hit was our feature construction, not the data.

The single largest lever so far was still data, not architecture: retraining through 2023 so the recency-weighted features carry the previous season lifted player correlation from 0.503 to 0.594 (+18%) and raised in-sample roster coverage from 13% to 63% of 2024 games.

Methods

Why the obvious metric misleads

Building this surfaced a lesson worth stating on its own. The natural metric for a per-plate-appearance model is held-out log-likelihood. It is the wrong yardstick, and following it selects the wrong model. One plate appearance is near maximum entropy (~1.495 nats), so every model scores near the floor; log-likelihood rewards getting the league-average distribution right and barely moves on whether the model tells individual players apart — which is the whole job of a world model.

Same 2024 test. JEPA has the best likelihood and the worst world model.
ModelPer-PA NLLPlayer-corr (avg)
JEPA (self-supervised + frozen probe)1.49 best0.10 worst
Causal transformer1.500.574
GRU1.490.578
MLP ensemble1.490.577
DiamondWorld v16 (SVI)—0.611

JEPA has the best likelihood and best raw accuracy of any model, and near-zero player differentiation: its self-supervised objective and frozen probe model the marginal dynamics and discard player identity. Best likelihood and worst world model are two views of one fact. Once differentiation is measured correctly, architecture barely matters (transformer, GRU, and MLP all land near 0.577; the generative SVI leads); the lever that moves the number is features, not a fancier network.

We pushed on JEPA to be sure the collapse was the probe and not the network. Fine-tuning JEPA's own encoder end-to-end lifts player-corr from 0.06 to 0.54 (about eight-fold), and training the identical encoder supervised from random init does marginally better still (0.56). So the collapse was the frozen self-supervised probe, and the self-supervised pretraining itself adds nothing here — it is a slightly worse starting point than random. The best-likelihood mode is still the worst world model, now shown within a single architecture family by changing only frozen versus fine-tuned.

Honesty

Does it beat the market?

No, and that is the honest result. Our strongest game-level model (v15) was run through a pre-game-only backtest on 2,355 real 2024 games with closing lines (v16 improves player rates, not the game-level verdict). Every market loses across the full edge sweep, the ROI does not improve as more edge is demanded (the opposite of a real signal), and the closing-line-value proxy is about zero.

Weighting the stake by confidence does not help and cannot: Kelly staking returns the identical ROI at 0.25×, 0.5×, and 1.0×, because sizing scales risk, not expected value. The model reports a large average “edge” (+8.7% to +18%) while hitting only ~48% — that confidence is miscalibration, not signal. Game lines are efficiently priced; a better player model adds no game-level information the market lacks. Its real gain lives in player props, which free data cannot test.

The contribution

Not “we built a good model,” but a generative object that answers counterfactual and distributional questions about baseball games no projection system can touch — roster what-ifs, lineup optimization, correlated tail-risk, uncertainty-aware win probability — with players measured directly against a standard baseline and against Steamer rather than assumed realistic, and a methods finding about why the natural evaluation metric misleads.

Limitations

What this is not

  • Roster coverage. The model sees through 2023, so 63% of 2024 games have all key players in-sample (up from 13%); demos use known-roster games, and minor-league priors extend to most debuts.
  • Bullpen timing. The simulator uses realistic hook distributions but generic timing; the betting evaluation uses pre-game-only bullpen information, since the actual relievers are look-ahead leakage.
  • Uncertainty scope. The bands are the fitted player-skill posterior plus game randomness; they do not include model-structure uncertainty.
  • Projection head-to-head. Steamer is now compared directly, from its real preseason-2024 release. ZiPS and THE BAT are not: no usable preseason-2024 capture of either exists, and we would rather omit them than estimate them.
  • The contact-quality result. First measured on a Marcel-style projection, then shipped and confirmed inside the model: v16 (v15 + contact quality) reaches 0.611, +0.017.
