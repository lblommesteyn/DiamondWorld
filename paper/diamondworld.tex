\begin{abstract}\small
\noindent
\textbf{Introduction.} Game-specific counterfactuals (what a lineup or starter change does to
\emph{this} game) are what teams actually decide on, but they are hard to validate: the counterfactual
outcome is never observed, so a simulator can report any number and no realized result contradicts it.
\textbf{Methods.} We build DiamondWorld, a plate-appearance-level generative model, an empirical rules
engine, and a full game simulator, and we propose a way to check its counterfactual forecast changes
without realized outcomes. Within a series the two teams and home field are fixed, so the game-to-game
change in an independent pregame market's implied win probability is driven by the starter and lineup.
We test whether the simulator's within-series forecast changes agree with the market's, using no game
outcomes and no look-ahead, and we calibrate the simulator's magnitudes to the market on an earlier
period and evaluate the frozen calibration on a held-out one. Every simulated input, including the
bullpen, is selected from information available before first pitch. \textbf{Results.} Across 2{,}343
within-series game changes (646 series, 30 teams, 2024), the simulator's and the market's forecast
changes correlate r = 0.51 (95\% CI 0.45 to 0.56, cluster bootstrap by series; sign accuracy 0.69), on
both pitching and hitting changes, beating a starter-and-lineup rate baseline fit to the market itself
(0.53 vs 0.42 on shared games, difference +0.12, 95\% CI 0.07 to 0.16), and the calibration factor
holds out-of-sample (frozen slope 0.96).
The same machinery values individual starting pitchers: the simulator's win probability added per start
agrees with the market's pricing of 175 starters at r = 0.65, and at r = 0.52 to 0.56 when the two are
measured on disjoint starts. Its per-game run distribution beats a summed model on proper scores (log
score 2.82 vs 2.95) and matches a league negative binomial while conditioning on the matchup. Its player
layer, averaged with a Marcel-style projection, is statistically level with Steamer (0.668 vs 0.671). Point
win probability is calibrated but not sharp, and the simulator is not intended to beat the market on
generic prediction.
\textbf{Conclusion.} We externally validate the direction and calibrated scale of game-specific baseball
counterfactuals against an independent forecaster, a check that has, to our knowledge, not been reported
before.
\vspace{1.2ex}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Marginal projection systems such as Steamer, ZiPS, THE\,BAT, and Marcel~\cite{tango} describe player
skill accurately, but they do not by themselves provide game-specific counterfactual distributions. A
front office decides on those: \emph{what does acquiring this pitcher do to our playoff chances},
\emph{does this lineup change the shape of tonight's game}, \emph{how confident should we be in this win
probability}. A game is a correlated sequence of plate appearances (PAs), so its mean is a sum of rates
but its \emph{shape} is not, and a summed projection cannot answer these questions.

A generative game simulator can, by construction. The difficulty is not building one, which has been
done for decades~\cite{bukiet,nakahara}. It is trusting it. A simulator will report that swapping in an
ace is worth a given number of win-probability points, but that number is internally generated and
never observed: the counterfactual game is played once, with the actual roster. This paper's
contribution is a way to check such counterfactuals externally. We ask two questions. \emph{(Q1) Can a
game-specific simulator produce counterfactual forecast changes that agree with an independent pregame
information aggregator, without using realized game outcomes?} \emph{(Q2) Does the simulator provide a
better conditional run distribution than independent or mean-only baselines?} A market's movement is a
forecast revision, not observed causal truth, so we treat market agreement as a \emph{validation
target}, not ground truth, and we are careful about that distinction throughout.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Player projection and simulation.} Marcel~\cite{tango} and its relatives regress recent
performance toward a mean; Markov-chain models of baseball~\cite{bukiet} and deep counterfactual
simulators~\cite{nakahara} produce run distributions and strategy estimates. A sufficiently rich Markov
model can itself generate correlated, game-specific distributions; our contribution is not that
simulation is new, but the external validation of its counterfactual sensitivities.
\textbf{Game-state value.} Continuous game-state value models, notably expected possession value in
basketball~\cite{cervone}, estimate the value of a state from a stochastic process; we produce
state-conditional estimates but validate discrete interventions against a market.
\textbf{Markets as forecasters.} Pregame prices from consensus sportsbook moneylines and from
prediction markets (Kalshi, Polymarket), de-vigged to implied probabilities~\cite{strumbelj}, are
strong information aggregators; we use them as an external forecast to validate against, not as causal
ground truth.
\textbf{Evaluation.} We use proper scoring rules and probability-integral-transform calibration for
count data~\cite{gneiting,czado}, classical win-probability baselines (Log5, Pythagorean)~\cite{james},
and, where we discuss interventions, the potential-outcomes framing~\cite{imbens}. The model is fit by
stochastic variational inference~\cite{hoffman,phan,jax}.

\section{The DiamondWorld model}
DiamondWorld is a pipeline of three components (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The \textbf{plate-appearance
model} is a generative model fit by stochastic variational inference~\cite{hoffman,phan}: for each PA
it draws a nine-way categorical outcome (K, BB, HBP, 1B, 2B, 3B, HR, out, reached-on-error) from a
softmax over a linear-plus-embedding score in batter and pitcher identity, a per-batter skill latent,
park, and game state (inning, base-out state, score, times through the order, cumulative pitch count).
Player identity enters through recency-weighted rate features and the skill latent; correcting a
minibatch scaling of the latent's divergence revived a posterior that had collapsed to its prior. The
locked specification adds \emph{contact-quality features}, a per-batter expected hit and home-run rate.
These are constructed once, from training-season batted balls only: we bin batted balls by exit
velocity and launch angle, take the league hit and home-run frequency in each bin, and average over
each batter's own batted balls to a static per-batter feature (Fig.~\ref{fig:heat}). They are
\emph{not} the current PA's launch measurements, so they are available before the PA; Table~\ref{tab:leak}
lists every feature and its availability, and removing the contact-quality features reverts the model to
its ablation ($0.624\!\to\!0.609$ cross-player correlation), a small change rather than a collapse,
confirming they are an additive, leak-free lever. A code audit then found and fixed three data-path
defects (an outcome-label mismatch, an attention mask that let the first position see later ones, and
unknown players sharing an index with a real one); with per-stat shrinkage of the rate features and a
season-to-season random-walk prior on the skill latent, this raises cross-player correlation to $0.644$
(paired gain $+0.019$ $[+0.007,+0.031]$).

\textbf{One locked model.} Every game-level result below uses this one specification, trained once
(seed 42) and simulated at $R{=}2{,}000$ replicas per game. An independent retraining from scratch on a
second machine reproduces its player differentiation ($0.643$ vs $0.644$). We also trained fifteen
variants on the corrected pipeline (other seeds, an optional sequence encoder over the game's earlier
plate appearances, optional joint training with a pitch-level model); none we scored paired against
the locked model differs from it on player differentiation (differences from $-0.005$ to $+0.007$, none
significant), so we did not select among them, and we report them only as a robustness check
(Table~\ref{tab:val}).

The \textbf{empirical rules engine} maps an outcome sequence to runs and base-state transitions; ported
from a validated Markov baseline~\cite{bukiet}, it reproduces test-season run totals to within half a
percent. The \textbf{game simulator} plays full nine-inning-plus-extras games with lineup cycling, a
bullpen, walk-offs, and the ghost-runner rule. Relievers follow a fitted \emph{hook model}, a logistic
hazard for pulling the starter in batters faced, times through the order, and runs allowed (held-out
AUC $0.889$), so the \emph{timing} of the hook is endogenous: it uses only pregame-fitted parameters
and in-simulation state. \emph{Who} relieves is also chosen pre-game: reading the relievers off the
completed game would leak the manager's in-game choices, which are endogenous to how the game went, so
each team's bullpen is instead the pitchers who appeared alongside that game's starter in strictly
earlier games, ordered by how often they did. The starter and the batting order are announced before
first pitch and are used as announced. An earlier version of these results used the realized bullpen;
every game-level number reported here is leak-free. In-game state is generated recursively as the game is played. The model is
trained through 2023 (rate features through
2024 for the later-season checks) and evaluated on 2024 unless stated. A held-out player-differentiation
ablation of architectures is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:arch}; the short version is that on that
metric, features mattered far more than architecture.

\section{What the simulator answers}
Because the object is generative, four families of questions fall out directly (Table~\ref{tab:cap}):
single-input counterfactuals (re-simulate the same game with one input changed), lineup construction
(reorder the same nine hitters), correlated tail-risk (the full game-total distribution, with a
within-game dependence a summed model lacks), and win probability with a decomposition of game
randomness from roster uncertainty (comparable in size over 2024, $4.6\%$ vs $4.9\%$ WP). A rich Markov
model can produce some of these; what is distinctive here is that the counterfactuals are
player-specific, generative, and, below, externally validated.

\section{Validating counterfactual forecast changes}
\label{sec:val}
The estimate ``change this starter, win probability moves by $X$'' is internally generated. We check it
against an independent pregame market's implied win probability, from consensus sportsbook closing
moneylines de-vigged to probabilities~\cite{strumbelj}, for the same teams under different pitchers. Within a series the two teams and home field are fixed, so the
game-to-game move in the market's implied win probability reflects changes in the starter, lineup, rest,
and other time-varying pregame information
(Fig.~\ref{fig:series}); if the simulator's within-series moves track the market's, its counterfactual
sensitivities have external support that uses \emph{no realized outcomes and no look-ahead}. We stress
that this validates the \emph{direction and scale of forecast changes}, not causal effects: the market
is an external forecaster, its move can reflect other same-day changes (rest, travel, weather, umpire,
late lineup or bullpen news), and it may share public inputs with the simulator, which would make the
agreement partly non-independent. We therefore report it as forecast-change agreement and quantify its
uncertainty carefully.

\textbf{Statistics} (Table~\ref{tab:val}). On 2{,}343 within-series game deviations (646 series, 30
teams), the simulator's and market's forecast changes correlate $r{=}0.51$, with a 95\% confidence
interval of $[0.45,0.56]$ from a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole series (Spearman $\rho{=}0.53$;
the logit scale gives $0.50$). Sign accuracy is $0.69$ $[0.67,0.71]$, well above chance. The result is
significant far beyond a permutation null (95th percentile $0.03$). The simulator explains about a
quarter of the market's game-specific revision ($R^2{=}0.26$): a real but partial share, which is the
honest reading. The result is not specific to the locked model. Every one of the fifteen variants is
significant alone, and pooling all sixteen gives $0.50$ $[0.44,0.56]$, the same as the locked model.
Replicas matter more than model choice: the locked model gives $0.48$ at $R{=}500$ and $0.51$ at
$R{=}2{,}000$, and variants simulated at $R{=}100$ give $0.31$ to $0.38$, because replica noise in each
game's forecast attenuates the correlation. At $R{=}2{,}000$ that noise is negligible (reliability
$0.95$).

\textbf{Calibration separated from evaluation, and the magnitude holds out-of-sample.} The
magnitude-calibration slope ($0.41$ raw, $0.43$ after correcting for the simulator's replica-sampling
noise, reliability $0.95$ at $R{=}2{,}000$) is fit on one random half of the series and evaluated, frozen, on
the held-out half. The held-out half is not only directionally right (sign accuracy $0.68$); its
magnitude matches. Its own OLS slope is $0.41$ (essentially the $0.42$ fit on the other half) with
intercept $\approx0$ and correlation $0.49$, and the decisive check is that regressing the held-out
market change on the \emph{frozen-calibrated} simulator change gives slope $0.96$ (target $1$) and
intercept $\approx0$: the calibration transfers rather than being refit on what it is evaluated on. The
held-out mean absolute error is $17\%$ below a predict-zero baseline. The raw simulator overstates
market-scale moves by about a factor of two, so a raw ``$+9.8$-point'' swing on a representative game
becomes about $+4.2$ points in market-calibrated units.

\textbf{The simulator beats a simple rate baseline.} Would a one-line index do as well? We build the
natural baseline from the same player table the simulator reads: the difference in the two starters'
allowed-run rates and in the two lineups' rate value, combined linearly with weights \emph{fit to the
market} on one half of the series and scored on the other. The simulator is fit to nothing. On the
1{,}690 game deviations where both are defined, the simulator correlates with the market's moves at
$0.53$ $[0.45,0.60]$ and the baseline at $0.42$ $[0.35,0.48]$; the paired difference is $+0.12$
$[+0.07,+0.16]$. The simulator also carries most of the signal: holding the baseline fixed, it still
explains the market (partial $r{=}0.38$ $[0.30,0.45]$), while the baseline adds little beyond the
simulator (partial $0.10$ $[0.04,0.16]$). Given the same player ratings, playing the game out is worth
more than summing them.

\textbf{Against Steamer-based pricing.} The market is partly built from public projections, so we
repeat the test with the same index built from Steamer's preseason projections instead of our own
ratings (1{,}341 deviations where Steamer covers both starters and at least eight batters a side).
Here the result is a tie: Steamer's index, again fit to the market, reaches $0.54$ against the
simulator's $0.50$ (difference $-0.04$ $[-0.09,+0.01]$). The two are complementary rather than
redundant: the simulator still explains the market with Steamer's index held fixed (partial $r{=}0.29$
$[0.20,0.38]$), and Steamer's index does with the simulator held fixed (partial $0.37$). Read together
with the previous paragraph, the simulator's engine adds value and its player ratings are what trail,
consistent with Sec.~\ref{sec:bench}, where our player layer is below Steamer's alone and level with it
only when averaged with a projection.

\textbf{Both pitching and hitting are priced.} Decomposing each within-series move into a pitching
channel (starters' allowed-run rate) and a hitting channel (lineup rate value), both carry independent
signal the market responds to (pitching $t{=}{-}17.6$, hitting $t{=}{+}7.3$), so the validation is not
pitcher-only.

\textbf{A later season, and a stale-input stress test.} Freezing the rate features and repeating the
within-series test on the 2025 sportsbook consensus, a season the features predate, gives $r{=}0.33$
(channel-level, using the model's inputs rather than a full re-simulation; see Limitations). As a
deliberate stress test we also run 2026-to-date against a \emph{prediction market} (Kalshi), a
different market mechanism, with rates now $2.5$ years stale (only about six of nine batters and half
the starters known): agreement attenuates to $r{=}0.12$, as expected. We do not claim this as clean
generalization; it shows the signal is real, appears across a sportsbook and a prediction market, and
degrades with stale inputs. A second prediction market (Polymarket) carries the same game markets but
its price histories did not pin cleanly to a single game's first pitch for a bulk pregame harvest, so
we report it as assessed rather than as a third estimate (see Reproducibility). These three evaluations
are not the same test and we keep them separate (Table~\ref{tab:evaltypes}): only 2024 is a full
end-to-end simulation; 2025 replays the frozen model's input sensitivities without re-simulating the
season, and 2026 is a stale-input stress test.

\section{From forecast agreement toward a causal reading}
\label{sec:causal}
The within-series design is a first difference that removes both team fixed effects and home
field, so it is closer to an identified intervention than a raw correlation. Under a
potential-outcomes reading~\cite{imbens}, for an ordered series we compare the simulator's
$do(\text{swap game }t\text{'s roster for game }t{+}1\text{'s})$ estimate against the market's
differenced forecast, and we stress the identifying assumption three ways. \emph{(i) Rest
control.} Adding the within-series difference in days of rest leaves the simulator coefficient
essentially unchanged ($0.412\!\to\!0.410$, $t{=}29$), so the effect is not a rest confound.
\emph{(ii) Negative-control placebo.} Pairing each simulator delta with a market delta from a
random other series gives mean correlation $0.00$ (99th percentile $0.05$); the observed $0.51$
is far outside that distribution. \emph{(iii) Sensitivity to unobserved confounding.} An omitted
differenced covariate would have to correlate at least $0.71$ with \emph{both} the simulator and
market deltas to explain the association; rest, the strongest observed such covariate, manages
only $0.07$. So no single plausible confounder accounts for the effect. We are explicit that
this is a design-based identification with stated assumptions, not a timestamp-clean starter-
scratch event study: the announced-probable-pitcher data required for that is not publicly
recoverable (the free schedule API overwrites the announced probable with the actual starter
post-game, leaving only four apparent 2024 scratches, which are artifacts). A clean event study
with proprietary intraday lines is the natural next step.

\section{Run distribution and where the simulator does not lead}
\label{sec:bench}
A validated counterfactual engine is only useful if its raw outputs are realistic, so we benchmark them
and report where the simulator trails (Table~\ref{tab:bench}).

\textbf{Run-total distribution.} Against an independent two-Poisson model with the identical per-game
means (only the shape differs), the simulator matches reality's spread (within-game total variance
$2.07\times$ the Poisson value, real $2.11\times$), with strictly better proper scores (log score $2.82$
vs $2.95$, paired difference $-0.137$ $[-0.163,-0.111]$; CRPS $2.39$ vs $2.45$, paired difference
$-0.060$ $[-0.075,-0.044]$~\cite{gneiting,czado}; negative favors the simulator) and better tail
probabilities (Fig.~\ref{fig:rundist}). It covers near nominal rates (achieved 80\% coverage $0.84$
$[0.83,0.86]$, nominal $0.80$) where Poisson badly under-covers ($0.67$), and this holds
\emph{conditional on the pregame expected total} ($0.83$ to $0.88$ across quartiles vs Poisson $0.65$ to
$0.70$), so the dispersion is game-conditional, not a single unconditional variance matched on average.
We do not claim to beat every baseline: a marginal league negative-binomial matches the simulator on
aggregate proper scores (log-score difference $-0.007$ $[-0.021,+0.007]$, CRPS difference $+0.019$
$[-0.006,+0.043]$, both straddling zero), but it assigns every matchup the identical distribution,
which the simulator's game-specific conditional distributions do not.

\textbf{Win probability.} Point win probability is honestly not the simulator's edge: it is calibrated
(ECE $0.033$) and beats the home-field base rate (log loss $0.683$ vs $0.692$), but it is less sharp
than a one-line team-strength baseline (AUC $0.58$ vs Log5 $0.62$). A per-PA process washes team talent
toward a coin flip, and DiamondWorld is not intended to beat a market on generic prediction; its value
is the conditional distribution and the validated counterfactuals.

\textbf{Player realism.} On 376 batters with at least 150 PA in 2024, the corrected model's cross-player
rate correlation is $0.648$, level with a Marcel projection augmented with the same contact-quality
feature ($0.651$) and below Steamer's real preseason release ($0.671$). The two are complementary: a
parameter-free average of their standardized predictions reaches $0.668$, statistically level with
Steamer (paired difference $-0.002$ $[-0.021,+0.016]$) and matching it on home runs ($0.654$ vs
$0.651$), while beating each of its parents ($+0.020$ $[+0.009,+0.032]$ over DiamondWorld alone,
$+0.017$ $[+0.005,+0.029]$ over Marcel). A fitted blend does worse ($0.660$), because least squares
targets squared error rather than correlation, so we report the unfitted average, which cannot
overfit (Fig.~\ref{fig:players}). The simulator itself consumes the model's rates, not the blend; the
blend shows that the model's player layer carries information a Marcel-style projection lacks.

\section{Decision studies}
\label{sec:decision}
The game-level validation says the simulator's forecast \emph{changes} are right in direction and, once
calibrated, in scale. A front office acts on a different object: the value of a specific player. We run
two decision studies, one on a lever the market prices and one on a lever it does not.

\textbf{What is a starter worth per start?} Rotation order, skipping a start, and above all trading
for a starter turn on one number: how much win probability a pitcher adds when he takes the ball,
relative to the alternatives on his own staff. The simulator answers it directly, and the within-series
design gives it an external check. In each series we express every game's forecast as a deviation from
the series mean, which removes both teams and home field; we credit the home starter with the deviation
and the away starter with its negative, so each credit is from the pitcher's own team's point of view; a
pitcher's value is his mean credit, computed separately from the simulator and from the market. Across
the 175 starters with at least ten 2024 starts (3{,}976 starts), the simulator's values agree with the
market's at $r{=}0.65$ $[0.54,0.75]$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:starters}). Because both are averages over the same
games, the stricter test measures them on disjoint starts: the simulator's value from a random half of a
pitcher's starts predicts the market's value from the other half at $r{=}0.56$ $[0.45,0.66]$ and, with
the halves swapped, $0.52$ $[0.40,0.63]$. The market agrees with itself across halves only at $0.79$,
so the simulator recovers most of what is recoverable. A calibration factor fit on half the pitchers and
frozen on the other half has slope $0.92$ (target 1) and intercept $-0.13$ points, and cuts the error of
a predict-zero baseline by $29\%$. In calibrated units the top of the 2024 list is what a front office
would expect (Burnes $+3.1$ points per start against the market's $+3.6$, Glasnow $+4.1$ vs $+5.5$,
Cole $+2.4$ vs $+2.8$), and over a 32-start season $+3$ points per start is about one win above the rest
of the pitcher's own rotation. The misses are informative rather than random. Five of the eight largest
overvaluations are veterans aged 31 or older whose prior-season rates had not yet caught up with their
decline (Paxton, Maeda, P\'erez, Walker, Verlander; the model has no aging curve). Four of the eight
largest undervaluations had little or no major-league history before 2024: rookies Skenes and Jones,
and the Japanese signings Yamamoto and Imanaga, whom the model sees only through a league-average
prior; two more are breakouts (Skubal and Ragans). Aging and pre-MLB evaluation are precisely the inputs a team
supplies from its own scouting, and the calibrated simulator is the layer that turns them into wins.

\textbf{Lineup construction.} Our second study takes a lever the market does not price, with the
winner's curse controlled. For each 2024 game we generate candidate batting orders of the home nine,
\emph{select} the order maximizing expected runs on one set of replica seeds, then \emph{evaluate} the
selected, actual, and a random order on an \emph{independent} set of seeds. The selection/evaluation
seed split is essential: the best of many candidates is inflated on its own selection seeds, so we never
score a choice on the seeds used to pick it. Honestly, a counterfactual lineup's realized outcome is
unobservable, so the reported gain is the simulator's own unbiased estimate of the lever. We are careful
about what is validated by what: the market evidence in \S\ref{sec:val} validates the general
counterfactual machinery, whereas the lineup number below is an application produced by that machinery
and is not itself market-validated. The two claims are kept separate.

Across 50 held-out 2024 games with 12 candidate orders each, the naive gain measured on the selection
seeds is $+0.47$ runs per game, which annualizes to an implausible $+76$ runs per season. Scored on
independent evaluation seeds, that gain collapses to $-0.03$ runs per game (95\% cluster CI $[-0.12,
+0.06]$), with only half of games improved and the selected order beating a random order by $+0.003$
versus the actual order's $+0.032$. The corrected estimate is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
We read this as two findings. First, the winner's-curse correction is doing real work: without the
selection/evaluation seed split we would have reported a large, entirely illusory improvement. Second,
batting-order optimization is a near-null lever at this granularity, consistent with the sabermetric
consensus that lineup order is worth at most a few runs across a full season. The methodological point of
the study is not that the simulator wins a decision, but that the same discipline used for the market
validation prevents it from manufacturing one. Together the two studies mark the boundary of use: the
simulator is trustworthy on levers the market can check, such as who pitches, and on levers it cannot
check it should be trusted only as far as a winner's-curse-controlled estimate allows. (The lineup
study was run on an earlier version of the simulator.)

\section{Limitations}
We are explicit about the boundaries. (1) The market is an external forecaster, not causal ground truth;
its move can reflect other same-day changes and it may share public inputs with the simulator, so the
$r{=}0.51$ is forecast-change agreement, not an identified causal effect. (2) $R^2{=}0.26$: the
agreement is real but partial. (3) The 2025 and 2026 checks use the model's rate features rather than a
full re-simulation of those seasons, and the 2026 slice is a stale-input stress test on an incomplete
season. (4) Raw counterfactual magnitudes overstate by about $2\times$ and must be market-calibrated.
(5) Point win probability is less sharp than a one-line baseline. (6) The simulator's mean total is
$0.45$ runs per game high ($9.08$ vs $8.63$); the proper-score comparisons mean-match before scoring,
so they test shape, and the offset largely cancels in within-series differences. (7) The replica count
matters: at $R{=}100$ the same kind of model scores $0.31$ to $0.38$, so the headline requires
simulating enough replicas per game ($R{=}2{,}000$ takes under ten minutes for a full season on one consumer GPU). (8) The starter valuations inherit the model's blind spots: no
aging curve, and only a league-average prior for pitchers new to the major leagues.

\section{Discussion}
The contribution is a way to \emph{externally validate the direction and calibrated scale of
game-specific baseball counterfactuals} when the counterfactual outcome cannot be observed, together with
a conditional run distribution that beats an independent baseline on proper scores and keeps its
coverage conditional on expected runs. To our knowledge, we did not find prior work that validates game-specific
baseball counterfactual sensitivities against independent pregame market forecast changes. For a front
office the implication is concrete: the simulator's market-calibrated value of a starting pitcher per
start agrees with how the market prices him, on starts it did not see, so acquisition and rotation
what-ifs rest on a validated engine, while levers the market does not price, such as batting order,
come with a winner's-curse-controlled estimate and no more. Its player layer is Steamer-grade when
averaged with a simple projection, and the known gaps (aging, pitchers new to the major leagues) are
exactly the inputs a team's own scouting supplies. All code and data-join pipelines are released
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

We compared the generative model against a per-PA MLP, a GRU, a causal transformer, and a
joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA)~\cite{lecun,assran} with variance-covariance
regularization~\cite{bardes} and a frozen probe, on identical inputs and target
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, Fig.~\ref{fig:metric}). Held-out per-PA log-likelihood is a poor yardstick: a PA
is near the empirical entropy of the league-average outcome distribution (about $1.495$ nats, well below
the nine-category maximum of $\log 9\approx2.197$ nats), so all models score near that floor. The
sharper metric is cross-player differentiation. A frozen JEPA probe attains the best likelihood and
near-zero differentiation; fine-tuning its encoder recovers it ($0.06\!\to\!0.54$) and from-scratch
supervised training does marginally better, so the collapse is the frozen objective and the
self-supervised pretraining adds nothing here. Once differentiation is measured correctly, the
transformer, GRU, and MLP cluster near $0.577$ and features (contact quality) move the number more than
architecture. Metric definitions, seeds, and per-run values are in the repository.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}
The repository contains the model, simulator, market-join pipelines, and every figure, with fixed
seeds, an environment lockfile, a data dictionary with source URLs and as-of dates, train/validation/test
manifests, rules-engine tests, and a one-command reproduction of the main figures and tables. Market
inputs that cannot be redistributed are provided as derived pregame probabilities with the exact
de-vigging and join logic and source timestamps, and the restriction is stated rather than papered over.
Key scripts: \texttt{scenario\_sim.py}, \texttt{run\_pregame\_sim.py}, \texttt{simulator\_benchmarks.py},
\texttt{validation\_stats.py}, \texttt{distributional\_stats.py}, \texttt{counterfactual\_validation.py},
\texttt{whatif\_channels.py}, \texttt{season\_market\_validation.py}, \texttt{lineup\_backtest.py},
\texttt{make\_figures.py}.
